# Opportunity definitions and arithmetic

Start with a problem and buying context, not a desired large market number. Define
geography/date, buyer/user/account/location/transaction units, segment criteria,
substitutes, budget owner, purchase frequency and the decision at hand. Market
research covers demand/population as well as competition; a competitor feature list
cannot establish a customer's unmet need or willingness to switch/pay.

An illustrative annual opportunity can be eligible distinct buying units multiplied
by an annual price/revenue assumption. State both units and assumptions, currency,
billing/tax/discount scope, adoption and churn/capacity where relevant. Convert
monthly versus annual figures explicitly; do not mix one-time and recurring totals.
Do not extrapolate a rate across incompatible regions/customer classes.

If sets overlap, reconcile at the actual buying-unit level or compute defensible
bounds/scenarios. Unknown overlap precludes simply summing segments; a partial
coverage estimate is not the whole market. Record sources/definitions/version and
whether counts are observed, modeled or hypothetical. A public list price is not
observed transaction revenue, price acceptance or marginal contribution.

Use ranges/sensitivity driven by real uncertainty, not arbitrary optimistic endpoints.
A TAM/SAM/SOM label does not validate input quality. Show which assumptions most
change the answer and the next affordable validation. Negative interviews, switching
costs, distribution constraints and existing adequate substitutes matter. Recruitment,
provider spend, ads and purchasing require actual scope; no invented customer access.

Primary starting point: [SBA market research and competitive analysis](https://www.sba.gov/counseling/plan-your-business/)
(reviewed 2026-10-14). Use the actual primary source for each current empirical input;
this guide and synthetic fixture do not supply real market-size or demand evidence.
